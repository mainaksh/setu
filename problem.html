The Problem

The world sends $900B home a year.
Middlemen skim 6% off the top.

Remittances are now the largest source of external finance for the developing world — bigger than foreign direct investment. Yet the fee to send them has barely moved in a decade. That gap is the opportunity.

6.36%Average cost to send $200 — Q3 2025, more than double the UN's 3% target
~15%Average cost when you send through a bank — the worst offender

The receiver

KERALA · INDIA

Waits 1–5 days, travels to a payout branch, and accepts whatever rate they're given. For millions, this transfer is the household's primary income. The friction is felt, not abstract.

Crucially, both ends are warm contacts — a sender and the family they love. That single fact is why a better product spreads along relationships instead of needing paid acquisition. (We'll return to this in Go-to-Market.)
Anatomy of the fee

Where does 6.5% actually go?

Almost none of it is "service." It's the cost of an old plumbing system: layers of correspondent banks, each taking a cut, plus a foreign-exchange markup hidden inside a rate you never get to see.

On a $270 transfer (AED 1,000)total ≈ $17.50
FX markup Sending fee Correspondent hops Payout / cash-out Compliance
FX markup (~$5). A spread baked into the rate — the most invisible and most resented charge.
Sending fee (~$5). A flat or percentage charge at the counter or in the app.
Correspondent hops (~$4). Each intermediary bank in the chain takes a slice and adds a day.
Payout (~$2). The cost of getting cash or a deposit to the receiver locally.

Illustrative breakdown consistent with World Bank cost-component analysis; exact split varies by provider and corridor.

Setu's insight: a regulated stablecoin replaces the entire correspondent-bank chain with a single near-free transfer. What's left is a thin FX spread and a local payout — which is how the all-in cost collapses below 1%.
It's not only the money

Three problems, not one.

💸

Expensive

6.36% average globally; up to 15% through banks. A direct, recurring tax on the lowest-income workers in the economy.

🐌

Slow

1–5 business days through correspondent banking. Money is "in transit" — useless to a family that needs it now.

🌫️

Opaque

The real cost hides in the exchange rate. The sender is told "no fee" while losing 3–4% on a marked-up rate.

Why this hasn't already been solved

The incumbents are structurally trapped.

This isn't a problem of nobody noticing. It's a problem of who can't move — each incumbent is locked into the very cost structure that makes them expensive.

✕ Banks

Run on correspondent banking — the multi-hop chain is their rail. They can't undercut a cost structure they're built on, and remittances are a low priority next to lending.

✕ Western Union & cash MTOs

A vast physical agent network is their moat — and their anchor. Cash handling, real estate and staff keep costs high; going cheap cannibalizes the network they depend on.

✕ Card networks

Interchange economics mean 2–3% is the floor, plus multi-day settlement holds. Their incentives point toward keeping fees, not killing them.

✕ Even the fintechs

Wise and Revolut genuinely improved pricing — but still ride banking rails and pre-funded float in every country, capping how low they can go and which corridors they can serve.

A new entrant on a fundamentally cheaper rail — regulated stablecoins — isn't competing on the same curve. It's a different cost structure entirely. That's the wedge.
